# The first computer bug

A moth, a relay and a logbook, 9 September 1947

Note:
The most famous bug in computing was a real insect. This deck tells what happened at Harvard that afternoon, why the joke worked, and why Grace Hopper's name is attached to it.

---

## Harvard, 1947

- Howard Aiken's Computation Laboratory at Harvard
- Mark I ran from 1944; Mark II was finished in 1947 for the US Navy
- Mark II was electromechanical: thousands of relays clicking open and shut

![The Harvard Mark I (IBM ASCC) at the Harvard Science Center](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e7/Aiken-IBM_Automatic_Sequence_Controlled_Calculator.jpg/1280px-Aiken-IBM_Automatic_Sequence_Controlled_Calculator.jpg)

Note:
The photo shows the Mark I, its older sibling; no freely licensed photo of the Mark II exists on Commons. A relay is a switch moved by an electromagnet, so a relay computer was full of tiny moving metal contacts, and full of places for something to get stuck.

---

## 15:45, relay #70, panel F

- Mark II kept giving wrong results
- The operators searched the machine, panel by panel
- In panel F they found a moth trapped in relay #70
- They pulled it out and taped it into the logbook

![A moth (poplar hawk-moth), for scale of the problem](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/2c/Poplar_hawk-moth_%28Laothoe_populi_populi%29_male.jpg/1280px-Poplar_hawk-moth_%28Laothoe_populi_populi%29_male.jpg)

Note:
The log entry is timed 1545. The moth in the photo is a stand-in: the real one is a small moth, flattened under tape, and still in the logbook.

## The logbook page

- 15:45: “Relay #70 Panel F (moth) in relay.”
- “First actual case of bug being found.”
- The moth is still taped there

![The Mark II logbook page with the moth taped to it](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e7/First_Computer_Bug%2C_1947.jpg/1280px-First_Computer_Bug%2C_1947.jpg)

Note:
The page of the logbook for 9 September 1947. Above the moth, the entries record the test runs; below it, "1545 Relay #70 Panel F (moth) in relay. First actual case of bug being found."

---

## The word was already old

- Engineers called faults “bugs” long before computers
- Thomas Edison used it in letters in the 1870s
- So the log is a pun: for once, a literal bug
- That is what “actual” means in the entry

![Thomas Edison in 1878](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/bb/Thomas_Edison%2C_1878.jpg/1280px-Thomas_Edison%2C_1878.jpg)

Note:
The moth did not give us the word. It gave us the best story about the word. Telephone and telegraph engineers already used "bug" for a hidden fault in a circuit.

---

> “Bugs” — as such little faults and difficulties are called — show themselves.
> — Thomas Edison, letter of 1878

Note:
From Edison's letter to Theodore Puskas, 13 November 1878: "It has been just so in all of my inventions. The first step is an intuition, and comes with a burst, then difficulties arise — this thing gives out and then that — 'Bugs' — as such little faults and difficulties are called — show themselves."

## Grace Hopper's story

- Joined the Harvard team in 1944, a Navy Reserve officer
- She did not find the moth, the operators on shift did
- She told the story for decades, in talks around the world
- That is why the bug is remembered with her name

![Grace Hopper, official US Navy portrait](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/ad/Commodore_Grace_M._Hopper%2C_USN_%28covered%29.jpg/1280px-Commodore_Grace_M._Hopper%2C_USN_%28covered%29.jpg)

Note:
Hopper (1906–1992) went on to build early compilers and shape COBOL, and retired from the Navy as a rear admiral in 1986. She loved the moth story and told it with the logbook as her proof; she never claimed to have coined the word.

---

## Debugging, ever since

- “Debug” was already used for aircraft and engines in the 1940s
- Programmers took it over: debuggers, bug trackers, bug bounties
- Almost every bug since is a mistake in the code, not in the hardware
- The moth was the rare one you could hold in tweezers

Note:
A good line to close the story: computers got faster by a factor of billions, and the debugging loop is the same as in 1947. Something misbehaves, you narrow it down, you find the cause, you write it down.

---

## Where the moth is now

- The Navy kept the logbook for decades
- In 1994 it went to the Smithsonian's National Museum of American History
- The moth is still taped to the page

![The National Museum of American History, Washington DC](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/96/National-Museum-of-American-History-Kenneth-E-Behring-Center-National-Mall-Washington-DC-04-2014.jpg/1280px-National-Museum-of-American-History-Kenneth-E-Behring-Center-National-Mall-Washington-DC-04-2014.jpg)

Note:
The logbook came to the museum from the Naval Surface Warfare Center in Dahlgren, Virginia. It is light-sensitive, so it is not always on display.
